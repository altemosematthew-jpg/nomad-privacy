How the numbers are computed, and what "best" means in each lens
The honest bottom line
No scanner can make options win almost every time. Options are priced close to fair, so the average trade loses roughly what it costs to get in and out. Win rate is easy to raise and doesn't mean profit: selling far out-of-the-money options wins most of the time and still loses money when the rare loss is large. This page shows expected value next to the win rate so the two can't be confused, and the Track record shows how its own picks actually did.
EV (expected value)
What the trade is worth on average if you hold to expiry, minus what you pay, as a percent of the money at risk. It uses a volatility forecast: 60% the market's implied volatility, 40% how much the stock has actually moved lately (20- and 60-day, with jump days such as earnings gaps removed). Every strike is scaled by the same factor, so the model never claims to know the skew is mispriced, and scheduled events keep the market's implied jump. Positive EV means the option is cheap against that forecast after costs. About −1% to −3% is just the cost of trading a fairly priced option.
Win % and the trade plan
The plan is: take profit at the target, stop out at the stop, otherwise close after the hold limit (or at expiry if sooner). Each contract is simulated on hundreds of price paths (thousands in the details panel), checked once a day at the close. Every leg moves with the same daily market shocks but at its own forecast volatility, plus its share of the market's implied earnings jump, and is repriced at its own market IV, which rises into a scheduled event and is crushed after it. That keeps the skew between strikes from showing up as fake profit: a fairly priced option or spread averages zero. Entries and exits assume a limit fill halfway between the mid and the natural price. Win % is the share of paths that close with a profit. Buying with a ±50% plan typically wins around 40–45%, and selling credit with a 50% target around 65–75%, with average losses bigger than average wins.
Grade
Absolute checks, not ranks. Liquid and tight. Expected value no worse than about −5% after costs. The plan wins often enough: about 42% for single buys, 40% for debit spreads, 60% for sales. 21–120 days for a buy, 14–60 for a sale. Decay under control for buys. No earnings inside the window. Not fighting the trend. For spreads, a reward worth the risk: at least 0.8× for debit spreads, a credit of at least 20% of the width for credit spreads. A clears every check, B misses one, C misses two. The Quality filter hides everything below B, so an empty table means nothing good is on offer right now. An A means a contract passes these checks, not that it has an edge: most A-grade buys are priced within a few percent of fair value. The forecast leans partly on realized volatility, which is usually below implied, so sales tend to look better than buys, and the simulated price paths have no crashes in them, so win rates for sales are on the optimistic side.
Vertical spreads
Debit spreads buy one strike and sell a further one: cheaper, less time decay and less IV crush than a single option, with the gain capped at the width. Credit spreads sell one strike and buy a further one: they win more often, and the max loss is known before you enter. For each strike in the delta range the page pairs the standard partner, about 1:1 reward to risk for debit spreads, about a third of the width collected for credit spreads. Robinhood needs options level 3 for spreads.
P(profit)
Chance the trade is profitable at expiry if held, at market implied volatility (Robinhood's own chance of profit when loaded from Robinhood). This is a different number from Win %, which follows the plan.
⚡ Earnings / event
A scheduled earnings date (Robinhood's calendar when loaded there, otherwise a built-in list) or a spike in the chain's forward variance between two expiries. Either way, the size of that spike is the market's implied move for the event. Buying through it bets on a bigger move than that, then IV collapses.
vs fair
Against the data source's own model price: Cboe's theoretical value, or Robinhood's mark in real-time mode (where it reduces to the half spread you give up).
Trend
Up when the price is above its 20-day average and the 20-day is above the 50-day, Down when both are reversed, Mixed otherwise. A bullish trade fights a downtrend and a bearish one fights an uptrend.
Consistency
How often a contract has looked under-priced across repeated scans. One cheap print is often a stale quote. Cheap scan after scan is more likely real.
Track record
Every trading day at about 2:45 PM ET, a scheduled job (no Claude involved) logs the top A-grade picks from the same engine and marks every open pick once a day: buys exit at the bid, sales buy back at the ask, using the default plan. It shows win rate, average result and how the model's predicted win rate compares with what actually happened. A few weeks of picks are needed before the numbers mean much.
My positions
Reads your open option positions from Robinhood (in your browser, with your login). For each one: P/L, the move needed to reach your cost, decay, chance of profit, the expected value of holding versus selling at the bid now, and the chance it trades back to your cost at some close before expiry. These are odds, not instructions.
Data
In the Claude version, Robinhood real-time quotes for the whole watchlist, re-quoted every minute while the market is open. On the public site, Cboe quotes delayed about 15 minutes, refreshed every 5 minutes. Nothing here is advice.

Not investment advice. Options can lose their entire premium, and short options can lose more than the premium received. Every probability here is a model estimate. Confirm the live price inside Robinhood before you submit an order, and size every trade so a full loss is one you can take.
